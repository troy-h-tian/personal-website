Freedom of Association and the LGBTQIA+ Campus Experience

A reflection on the most critical issue facing LGBTQIA+ students on campus — arguing that the freedom to curate one's community, beyond demographic affinity structures, is essential to holistic queer student wellbeing.

All names used in this response were changed for privacy.

I believe the most critical issue for LGBTQIA+ students on campus is that of the freedom of association, by which I mean the right of a person to curate the individuals with whom they associate, as well as to create and organise groups dedicated to common interests. I'm extremely grateful to have found queer groups where I can feel at home, including the CRC's own QTBIPOC Space as well as other clubs like the Lavender Alliance (made for LGBTQ+ students of Chinese descent). And while these groups' very explicit purposes and stated affinities make it easy for some people to connect within them, the journey isn't always so simple for others.

My friend Ace, a trans nonbinary POC, struggles with social anxiety given their ASD and other personality traits. Despite my many invitations to QTBIPOC Space, they feel it'd be too intimidating to speak their mind even in what they know will be a supportive environment; despite no fault from the organisers, associations based solely upon shared demographics can't accommodate for every potential attendee. Similarly, my queer Chinese American friends Ronan and Emily don't come with me to Lavender Alliance, partially because it's mostly international students and participation in activities depends upon Mandarin fluency (even I struggle to keep up there). I haven't heard of a space specifically for queer American-born Chinese.

Conversely, though, examples also abound of positive experiences found in not-necessarily-expected places. My stereotypically effeminate gay roommate absolutely loves his frat, and Emily's social experiences mostly come from within straight male-dominated computer science clubs. As a normally "straight-passing" Asian male, I'm also privileged enough to be taken seriously among engineering majors, and many of my friends are cishetero men in STEM. My lesbian friend Imelda, who says her SoCal hometown is like "Mexico 2.0", voluntarily remains close with her devout Catholic friend who "doesn't support" homosexuality. I myself, as a Buddhist, enjoy going to Bible study and learning about various forms of Christianity in detail, even as my queer roommates proclaim themselves "slightly Christian-phobic" due to its friction with their identity.

I name all of these examples not to diminish the importance of safe/affinity spaces explicitly meant for certain demographic groups, nor to valuate my and my friends' everyday social lives over serious topics like bullying, harassment, sexual/gender-based violence, and assault. Advocacy and resolutions for these problems are extremely important, and involve far more than just the efforts of interns or of the CRC. It remains true, though, that many of the issues of mental and emotional health that underscore queer college students' struggles depend very much upon the community they're able to find at school, or the lack thereof. Even bullying and harassment themselves are partially enabled by a lack of freedom of associativity; my "Christian-phobic" roommates deal often with casual queerphobia from the classmates, professors, and people they must interact with on a daily basis.

The choice is what matters — not just to attend or not attend a Bible study or queer club meeting, but also to feel supported in one's journey to finding individuals and groups that truly make you feel at home. I love when I'm able to introduce Ace to friends if they're struggling to meet new people, but they are just one of many examples of LGBTQIA+ students who are more than just their identity and the boxes they check. Every student like them, like Ronan, Emily, Imelda, my roommate, and even myself, deserve programs, advocacy, and overall effort that can both support and go beyond existing affinity structures to things increasingly holistic.
