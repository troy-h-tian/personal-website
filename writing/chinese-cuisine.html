The Semantic Space Between: The Timeless Symbolism of American Chinese Cuisine

Chinese food is a staple of many an American dining-out experience today. Whether it’s Michelin star-rated bistros or all-you-can-eat hot pot, Panda Express styrofoam giants or local family-owned buffets, the scope of Chinese cookery is often taken for granted in its integrity to the American ethnic-cuisine landscape. This has not always been the case, however; the notion of the “typical Chinese restaurant” encompasses a rich and storied past of discrimination, sacrifice, and the endless perseverance of people towards a better life. The history and development of the American Chinese food industry are emblematic of the complex journey of the Chinese American community.

The first Chinese immigrants in the mid-19th century were manual labourers, mostly young men from specific, concentrated parts of southern China. Early Chinese immigrants to the US were mostly from Taishan, a rural, coastal county in the province of Guangdong, where an established cultural precedent for voyages abroad combined with ethnic conflict and an economic depression prompted considerable emigration. As Mr. David R. Chan, who has dined at over 8,000 Chinese restaurants, observed: “it was like if all the Americans in China were from a small town 100 miles outside Los Angeles. Very underrepresented. As a result, early American-Chinese food tended to be homogenous” (Feng).

The mostly-Chinese clientele of these pioneering establishments greatly influenced the foods they served. Early Chinese restaurants were more often than not frequented by an internal market of Chinese patrons who enjoyed traditional dishes like salted fish, webbed duck feet, pig stomach, intestines, and fish heads. The Chinese food industry in America arose from practical, popular need augmented by cultural tendencies; Chinese who saw themselves as guests in a foreign country found great value in the preservation-through-consumption of their original culinary identity.

Throughout the mid-to-late 19th century, a burgeoning Chinese community in major cities began to establish an equally expansive restaurant industry as Chinatowns gave them a place of their own in American society. “Chinese food’s transplantation paralleled the creation of Chinese communities. In major cities in California, such as Sacramento and San Francisco, food businesses represented a vital sector in the Chinese ethnic economy” (Y. Chen). These restaurants attracted both Chinese and non-Chinese patrons with all-you-can-eat meals at a reasonable price. According to one miner of the period, the Chinese restaurants were the best eating houses in San Francisco.

The demographic congregation and social specialisation of Chinese immigrants into food service gave American Chinese cuisine a unique degree of commodification, standardising it through simplification to fit American tastes and establishing its cultural perception as consistent and reliable. At a time when ethnic Chinese’s perception by Whites as strange and exotic foreigners precluded their free participation in most of society, the delicious quality of their food was often the only path to potential racial tolerance.

The early 20th century saw a further relegation of Chinese into the restaurant business specifically, as discrimination and racialised cultural perceptions reinforced restaurants as the Chinese American community’s only economic lifeline. Chinese were driven out of what historical manual-labourer communities still remained in rural territories by vehement and violent White nativists. During the 1885 Rock Springs Massacre in Wyoming, 28 Chinese were killed, 15 wounded, and all 79 of the shacks and houses in Rock Springs’ Chinatown were looted and burned. In the same decade, the Chinese Exclusion Act was passed, “effectively prohibiting the entry of laborers from China” and “institutionalizing discriminatory practices against Chinese immigrants who were denied various civil rights and subject to extensive restrictions in the U.S. labor market” (Chen & Xie).

Chinese restaurants, being among the only places these institutional victims could work, became economically essential to the Chinese community. Visiting the local Chinatown and patronising its establishments “became a national pastime around the turn of the 20th century” (Y. Chen), in large part due to the exotic yet delectable food its restaurants provided — reinforcing negative stereotypes of Asians as “perpetual foreigners” but at the same time affording Chinese-owned businesses a vitally necessary customer base.

World War II made China an ally and Chinese culture romantic. Later on, the 1965 Immigration and Nationality Act removed racist quotas on Asian immigration, creating a surge in migrants from mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong — many of them refugees from the Chinese Civil War. From this, both the then-latest iterations of Cantonese cuisine as well as an array of regional Chinese cuisines were brought to the United States, again demonstrating Chinese restaurants’ representation of the demographics of Chinese immigrants as a whole.

The civil rights movement’s fostering of increased social and cultural acceptance of Asian Americans also allowed at least some amount of room for authenticity to flourish. Chinese restaurant owners increasingly “appealed to external audiences by playing on the idea of foreignness. They created niche markets of Americans who liked the idea of ‘exotic’ foods, ushering in an acceptance of more traditional dishes” (Chang et al.). The ethnic Chinese among them were finally given the economic freedom and social licence to live within both identities simultaneously, including through food.

American Chinese cuisine now finds itself familiarly established and yet also constantly evolving, as Chinese continue to form identity and community within the United States’ melting pot of a country. Like their predecessors of over a century-and-a-half ago, Chinese immigrants establish restaurants as staples of their ethnic community, serving a taste of their beloved home province wherever they decide to take root. Unlike those early years, Chinese restaurants’ cookery today is no longer homogenous in the slightest.

Shen Yan, owner of the Asian eating house Y Noodle & Bar in Providence, Rhode Island, remarked of her menu that “it’s all, like, combined … you have to combine [the food of] all the other province.” In addition to ramen noodles, characteristic of Yan’s northern Chinese background, Y Noodle also serves soup dumplings, seafood boils, and much more — a pan-Chinese synthesis of its staff’s varied origins.

Yan offers her own perspective on the authenticity debate: “I think people here are trying to find authentic, like, even Chinese, Japanese, all authentic food, but they don’t really know what actually is authentic.” The cultural legacy of Chinese restaurants serving enjoyable, affordable, and reliable food to a diverse American population is undoubtedly still honoured into the present day, whether they are “Chinese Chinese,” “American Chinese,” or, like many Chinese Americans themselves, something nestled snugly in the semantic space between.

The odyssey of the American Chinese food industry, from humble beginnings to modern-day ubiquity, tells the extraordinary third-culture tale of an unabashedly third-culture people. The importance of restaurants to the cohesion and unity of the Chinese population is unparalleled, and their symbolism of the immigrant story’s historical evolution unmatched. Through industriousness, solidarity, perseverance, enterprise, and, above all, community, Chinese Americans have established both themselves and their cuisine as an integral part of United States society, making an undoubtedly delicious mark on the country they so unforgettably call their own.
